**Stina Baudin**

Stina Baudin (she/her) is a Haitian-Canadian research based visual artist presently based in Montreal and Detroit Metro Area. She studied at Concordia University in 2012 and the The Academy of Fine Arts in Brussels in 2019. She is a 2025' MFA graduate and a Gilbert Foundation Fellow from Cranbrook Academy of the Arts.   

Using archival data, sculpture, sound, text, textiles and installations, she creates works that challenge dominant narratives and singular ways of telling Black peoples contested histories. Bringing together these forms through traditional craft methods and innovative media technologies, Baudin underscores the importance of multiplicity in telling tales. Her work bridges the divisions between traditional narrative and data, truth and myth. In this way, her work seeks to give expression to the varied bodies of knowledge that emerge through geographical movement, ritual, ancestry and history.   

Baudin is a 2023/24 and 2024/25 recipient of the Gilbert Foundation Scholarship and the Maxwell/ Hanrahan Foundation Materials Award (US) from Cranbrook. She has participated in residencies at ZK/U Berlin (DE), the Banff Centre for Art and Creativity (CA), Pocoapoco (MX), and CultureHub (NYC). Recent exhibitions include the Toronto Biennial of Art and Momenta Biennale (CA). Her work has been supported by the Canada Council of the Arts, Holon Berlin and CALQ (Conseil des Arts du Québec).

**Guadalupe García**   
Guadalupe García is a historian of Cuba and the Caribbean with interests in cities, colonialism, and urban geographies. Her first book, Beyond the Walled City: Colonial Exclusion in Havana (University of California Press, 2016) is based on archival research in Cuban, North American, and Spanish collections. It explores the relationship between city space and Spanish colonial rule in Cuba from the founding of the city in the early sixteenth century through the end of the U.S. military occupation in 1902. It reveals urban-based patterns of imperial rule and argues that colonialism in the Caribbean—like neocolonialism beyond it—was not simply concentrated in the institutions, disciplines, and discourses of the Spanish empire, but rather established through the use of city space and built environment. Professor García’s work has appeared in the Journal of Latin American Studies and Cultural Studies. Her fellowships and awards include a Distinguished Fellowship at the Advanced Research Collaborative of the CUNY Graduate Center and research and digital fellowships at the John Carter Brown Library in Providence, Rhode Island. She has also held a Transatlantic Research Fellowship at the University of Warwick in the UK.

**Sophia Monegro**   
Sophia Monegro is a literary scholar working at the intersection of Black Women’s Intellectual
History, Dominican Studies, and Digital Humanities. She earned her PhD in the Department of
African and African Diaspora Studies at The University of Texas at Austin and she is a
Postdoctoral Fellow with the Center for Race, Ethnicity, and Equity at Washington University in
St. Louis. Her dissertation, Shadow Scholars: Black Women’s Social Thought in Santo Domingo,
1492-1900, traces African and African descendant women’s activism and intellectualism on the
Indigenous island of Ayiti, today’s Haiti, and the Dominican Republic. This multimodal
dissertation repositions entrepreneurial healers, savvy market women, plantation managers, and
working-class organizers as organic intellectuals to argue that the principles embedded in Black
women’s daily practices in colonial Santo Domingo constitute the intellectual pillars of Black
Feminism in the Americas. The Fulbright Program in the Dominican Republic, the Mellon
Foundation, and the American Association of University Women have supported Monegro’s
scholarly research. Monegro is currently serving as the Primary Investigator on an ACLS Digital
Justice Seed Grant for the creation of Cimarronas: A Black Women’s Archive of Ayiti-Quisqueya,
a trilingual digital platform aimed at making Black Women’s histories in the Caribbean accessible
to diverse audiences using interactive animations. Her digital projects, archival preservation work,
and revisionist public history activism are grounded in the community-based and material needs
of Black Dominicans and African American descendants in the Dominican Republic and its
diaspora.

**Rohan Sagar**   
Rohan Sagar is a Guyanese ethnomusicologist, digital humanities scholar, and cultural heritage
practitioner whose work bridges Indigenous knowledge systems, computational analysis, and arts
education. He is the founder of the Harpy Eagle Music Foundation and lead architect of Digital
Heritage GY, a national initiative that digitizes, analyzes, and recontextualizes Guyana’s diverse
musical traditions using tools from computational ethnomusicology and digital pedagogy.

Sagar’s projects span audio-visual archiving, machine learning, and curriculum design, with a
focus on underserved and Indigenous communities. His groundbreaking Decoding the Baboon Dance [https://digitalheritagegy.com/Digital_Humanities/baboon-dance-/baboon-dance---a-
music-curriculum-for-indigenous-education] project applies spectral and rhythmic analysis to Indigenous music for integration into formal education systems, addressing longstanding equity gaps in Caribbean music education. He is also the creator of the Guyana National Anthem Digital Humanities Project, [https://digitalheritagegy.com/Digital_Humanities/the-national-anthem-of-the-co-operative-republic-of-
guyana/index] which uses waveform visualization, lyric sentiment analysis, and multilingual translation—including Spanish and Indigenous language adaptations—to enhance cultural inclusion, national identity, and civic education. This initiative has been widely recognized for its role in making national symbols more accessible and analytically rich for students and educators.

A graduate of Future Generations University (M.A. in Applied Community Change and Conservation), Sagar’s work combines community-based research with cutting-edge digital methods. He has collaborated with schools and cultural groups across Santa Rosa, Lethem, Buxton, and Bartica to co-create open-access educational materials rooted in cultural heritage. Through his presentations and digital platforms, Sagar champions a decolonial and pluralistic approach to music education, leveraging technology to ensure cultural sustainability and equity in the Global South.

Clifton Sorrell is a sixth-year Ph.D. candidate studying early modern Atlantic Slavery and the
African Diaspora in the early Caribbean. Centering on seventeenth-century Spanish Jamaica, his
dissertation examines how Black free, enslaved, and cimarrón communities navigated and

Brown Library. Clifton is also an Advisory Council dissertation writing fellow at the McNeil
